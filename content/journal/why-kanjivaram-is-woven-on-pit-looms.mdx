The first thing you notice, standing next to a Kanchipuram loom, is that the weaver is sitting *below* the floor. Their legs disappear into a pit that's roughly three feet deep, and the treadles — the pedals that lift and drop the warp threads — hang from the ceiling on ropes.

This isn't nostalgia. It's physics.

## Why the pit matters

A pure zari Kanjivaram sari has three separately woven pieces: the body, the *pallu* (the decorated end that drapes over the shoulder), and the border. The three are joined with a specialised interlocking weave called *korvai* — you can hear the tiny snap of it when the sari is pulled taut. Getting korvai clean requires a very specific tension on the warp, which requires the weaver to shift their weight *downward* into the pedals rather than pressing forward. In a pit loom, gravity does most of that work.

Above ground, you can get more speed. Below ground, you get better *fall*.

## What it costs

A single Kanjivaram sari with an all-over zari body takes 15 to 25 days on a pit loom. On a mechanised jacquard, you can be through 30 saris in the same time — but the silk won't sit the same way on the body. The drop is heavier, the border sits crooked when you sit down, and after a wash the zari dulls faster because the mechanical loom pulls the metallic thread through at a harder angle.

The premium you pay for a pit-loom Kanjivaram isn't for the silk. It's for the twenty days.
